## Method

0. Purpose first: open the creative-brief skill and write the one-line brief (goal, the ONE thing it sells, for whom, the message, the action, where it runs). A good-looking piece that sells nothing in particular has failed.
1. Words. Write exactly three blocks and nothing else. The message is one sharp selling line of at most 7 words that a stranger gets in 3 seconds; its first two words carry the meaning. The supporting line is one short sentence of proof or how, never a repeat of the message. The action is ONE thing to do, in a few words. Add a small line above the message only when it carries news the message lacks ("launching on Google Play"); for an announcement that small line IS the news and is never dropped. No phrase appears twice across the blocks: the same launch phrase in the label, the headline and the button was the founder's first complaint. No emojis, no exclamation marks, no "best", no invented number; a gap is marked "[you tell us: ...]".
2. Real material first. Use `search` for the thing promoted, then look for the business's own pictures: project, product and place photos in the brand library, approved pictures, and real app screens (a screenshot filed as an app screen keeps its crop and the box of the part that matters). Real work sells better than a made-up scene and costs nothing. Ask the owner for one when none fits ("send two photos of a finished room"). A generated scene is the last resort, and it must be specific: this business, its city, the people it serves, the phone's back to the camera, no lettering, never a stock office or a laptop on a desk.
3. People. A face needs that person's consent, which the library does not record: prefer pictures without faces, or with the person turned away or cropped. Generated people are illustrative.
4. Pick the layout by the job, from the library (the poster tool chooses by the brief, never repeats the last one, and takes a preference):
   - device-hero: an app launch or a launch announcement; the real screen in one large phone under the words.
   - scene-device: an app in someone's day; a photo of the person and place, the screen's key part lifted out as a readable card.
   - feature-callout: one feature shown exactly; the screen with its key part magnified in place.
   - editorial-frame: premium services and finished work; one real photo framed like a magazine page, the project's name small.
   - gallery-trio: proof by work; three of the business's own photos with their names.
   - photo-full-bleed: one photo strong enough to carry the idea; words low on a measured shade.
   - split-panel: a selling line next to a photo, the words on solid brand colour.
   - offer-card: a real offer the owner gave, the offer itself as the hero.
   - big-type: five words or fewer that need no picture.
   - quote-card, before-after, date-announcement, hiring-card, local-place, event-poster, carousel-cover: only when the brief has a real quote, a real before and after, a date, a role, an address, an event or a carousel.
5. Reels follow the spoken-reel method (reel-script, reel-scene-list) and these rules: the hook line is on screen from the first frame and gone by 2.6 seconds; each scene's line appears a few words at a time at reading pace, so the reel works on mute; real app screens appear in a phone with the camera pushing in on the part the line is about; the business's own photos fill the frame; generated clips start from a real frame and only move the camera; everything sits inside Meta's reel safe zone (14% top, 35% bottom, 6% sides); the end card carries the real logo and ONE action. Music and clips cost credits: say the price and wait for the owner's OK.
6. What the tools do after you hand over: the composer measures every word (fit, lonely last words, contrast against the pixels behind it, the story's covered band), Growth Hacker reviews each option against the checklist and the composer revises it, then Fact Checker checks every word. An option that never passed the review is still shown, with what the review still wanted. Pass that on in plain words; never call it finished when it is not.
7. Hand the plan over with `propose_action` (kind content_draft, body = the three blocks, the pictures to use by name, the layout and one line on why) so it waits for the owner's OK, or give it to the Chief to make.

## House style

The plan in a few lines the owner can read at a glance:

> Brief: sell Interior Design to homeowners in Raipur; action: book a site visit.
> Message: "Rooms planned around how your family lives" | Support: "Interior design in Raipur, from the first sketch to the last cushion." | Action: "Book a site visit at avsatelier.in"
> Pictures: The Penthouse bedroom, Manidweepam bedroom, Ramnani Residence living room (the studio's own photos, no faces)
> Layout: editorial-frame (one finished room, framed), with gallery-trio as the second option (three projects as proof).

## Done well

- [ ] One message, one supporting line, one action; no phrase said twice; the news of an announcement kept.
- [ ] The pictures are the business's own real work or real app screens wherever they exist; any generated scene is specific to the business and its place.
- [ ] No stranger's face without consent; no lettering, logo or app screen drawn by a model.
- [ ] The layout fits the job and is not the one used last.
- [ ] A reel's hook is on frame one and its words are timed and inside the safe zone.
- [ ] What the review still wanted is told to the owner.

## Common mistakes

- A label, a headline and a button that say the same thing three times.
- A generated stock office or a laptop scene for a business that has real photos of its work.
- Every post in the same layout, so the grid looks like one template repeated.
- A whole phone screen at phone size, which nobody can read: lift out the part that matters.
- A pill button on a static post that cannot be tapped: write the action as plain words.
- A reel made of brand-colour cards with a slogan, and no real product in it.
